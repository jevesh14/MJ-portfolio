Music timbre modelling

A compact model of harmonics

A trombone and a saxophone can play the same note yet sound different because their overtones have different strengths and phases. Recording every overtone independently takes many values, especially when the sound changes from the attack to the decay. I developed a more compact description using complex singularity atoms, mathematical components that capture a related pattern across several harmonics at once.

Each atom has parameters controlling how quickly higher harmonics weaken and how their waves align. Rather than fit every value separately, I used a matrix-pencil initialiser for suitable integer-type components and variable projection, which solves the component strengths by linear least squares before adjusting the nonlinear shapes. That reduces the nonlinear search from five to three unknowns per component, making the model easier to fit without discarding the relationship between harmonics. The derivation appears in my mathematics paper.

Reconstruction and prediction

Allowing the atom parameters to change over time made it possible to follow how a note begins and fades. I tested reconstruction on 72 isolated tones from the University of Iowa instrument recordings, comparing the resulting harmonic spectra with the original measurements.

Using 170 model values instead of 1,088 direct coefficient values—about 84% fewer—the representation retained 93.6% of harmonic coefficient energy, with normalised complex reconstruction error of 0.253. In separate synthetic tests, I fitted the first 24 harmonics and predicted the next 24, obtaining 1.5% median relative error on the withheld harmonics.

Parameter identifiability

A compact fit is only useful if its parameters can actually be determined from the recorded sound. I derived identifiability conditions for when the harmonics determine the atoms apart from equivalent reorderings, then examined the coefficient-map Jacobian to see how small changes in atom parameters affect the predicted spectrum. The same fitted parameters can also be used to calculate quantities such as spectral centroid, the energy-weighted average frequency, without reconstructing every harmonic separately.

Vibration-driven rope slip

Predicting the slip boundary

When a rope is wrapped around a vibrating drum, it can move near one end while staying firmly gripped farther around the drum. Knowing where the slipping region ends matters for assessing how securely a tensioned line holds a load in winches and rope drives, where unexpected motion can change the tension or shift the load. I derived an equation for this boundary rather than treating the whole wrapped rope as either sliding or stationary.

The model combines capstan friction, which describes how tension changes along a wrapped rope, with preslip contact stiffness, the resistance to small movements before full sliding. Balancing the vibration-driven force against this frictional resistance gives a closed-form logarithmic law that predicts how the boundary moves as vibration amplitude or holding tension changes.

The physical experiment

To test the equation, I used a braided polyester rope wrapped four times around a torsionally vibrating aluminium drum. The experiment comprised 84 physical runs across 14 conditions, with six repetitions per condition and separate sweeps of vibration amplitude and holding tension.

I calibrated the drum geometry, friction, drive amplitude and contact stiffness independently of the measured slip boundaries. Optical tracking then recorded movement along the rope, and regression estimated local creep rates so that the predicted boundary could be compared with an experimental one.

Identifying a measurable boundary

Small movements in the rope do not always reveal a clear transition from slip to grip. I therefore compared five possible spatial movement patterns using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which rewards a good fit while penalising unnecessary model parameters.

A boundary was accepted only when the localised model had a BIC advantage of at least 10, the measured motion exceeded three times the camera noise floor, and the inferred position was clear of the measurement edges. These checks reduced the risk of mistaking general drift or measurement noise for a slip front.

Prediction against measurement

Across eight amplitude–tension settings, the equation predicted the boundary angle with 0.117 rad (6.7°) root-mean-square error. Its mechanical constants had already been measured, so the boundary positions were not used to tune the prediction.

The measured dependence on log vibration amplitude was 9.54 ± 0.74 rad, compared with 10.10 ± 1.45 rad predicted. For log holding tension, the values were −5.56 ± 0.50 rad measured and −5.56 ± 0.62 rad predicted. Their agreement across two independently varied inputs supports the force-balance model under the tested conditions.

Physics-informed spirometry

Incomplete expiratory recordings

A spirometry test uses the amount of air exhaled in the first second (FEV₁) relative to the total forced exhaled volume (FVC) to help identify airway obstruction. If the patient stops blowing too early, FVC can be underestimated and the FEV₁/FVC ratio artificially increased, potentially concealing obstruction. I investigated whether the missing end of the exhalation could be estimated without treating every incomplete test as reliable.

I decoded NHANES flow–volume recordings at 100 samples per second, aligned the start of exhalation by back-extrapolation, and fitted a bounded two-compartment model representing fast and slow lung emptying. Complete recordings provided a reference for testing how accurately the model reconstructed the missing volume.

Reliability-based selection

A fitted curve is not automatically a trustworthy clinical estimate. I therefore compared the physiological reconstruction with ridge regression and built a reliability selector using curve-fit quality, remaining volume change, recording duration and agreement between the two approaches.

At 75% coverage, the selector gives results for the three-quarters of recordings judged most reliable and flags the rest for repetition. I used participant-separated cross-fitting to keep a person’s records from appearing on both sides of model evaluation, then tested the frozen method on separate NHANES survey cycles.

Independent evaluation

On naturally incomplete recordings, reconstruction reduced missed obstruction from 42.7% to 6.7%. At 75% accepted-case coverage in independent NHANES Cycles E and G, sensitivity reached 96.0% and 96.1%, with specificity of 99.7% and 99.8%. Cohen’s kappa was 0.952 and 0.959, indicating high agreement beyond chance.

Compared at the same accepted-case coverage, the physics-informed selector improved kappa over a ridge-feature selector by 0.019 and 0.030 in the two cycles. Paired bootstrap intervals excluded zero, supporting the value of the physiological features for deciding which incomplete recordings could be assessed reliably.
