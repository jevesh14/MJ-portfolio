Music timbre modelling

1. Instrument timbre and harmonic structure

In music, two instruments, such as a trombone and a saxophone, can play the same note yet sound different because their overtones differ in strength and timing. Describing each overtone separately requires many values, especially when following how a note begins and fades.

To describe these sounds more compactly, I developed a model using complex singularity atoms, mathematical components that each represent a linked pattern across several overtones. A component’s depth controls how quickly higher overtones weaken, while its angular position controls their phase, or how the waves align.

2. Parameter identifiability and recovery

By allowing the parameters to follow time-varying trajectories, the model follows a note’s onset and decay. I derived identifiability conditions, specifying when measured harmonics determine the component parameters, and used the coefficient-map Jacobian, which measures how harmonics respond to parameter changes, to examine local recovery.

3. Harmonic reconstruction and prediction

The fitting method uses variable projection, calculating each component’s strength by linear least squares before numerically adjusting its shape. Separating the linear and nonlinear steps reduces the harder search from five to three unknowns per component.

When evaluated on 72 Iowa instrument tones, 170 model values retained 93.6% of harmonic coefficient energy, compared with 1,088 values in the direct description, using about 84% fewer values. Normalised root-mean-square reconstruction error was 0.253, with a 12.75 dB signal-to-noise ratio for the harmonic reconstruction.

To test whether the model could predict overtones it had not received, synthetic trials fitted the first 24 harmonics and withheld the next 24. Predictions for the withheld harmonics had 1.5% median relative error, supporting the use of shared components to describe relationships across the spectrum.

Vibration-driven rope slip

1. Vibration-induced slip in wrapped ropes

When a rope is wrapped around a vibrating drum, it can slide near one end while remaining firmly held farther around it. Predicting where this slip stops helps assess how securely a tensioned line holds a load in winches and rope drives, where unwanted movement can release tension or shift the load.

To predict that position, I derived a logarithmic force-balance law for the boundary between slipping and gripping contact, where the force transmitted by vibration equals the frictional resistance holding the rope against the drum.

The law combines capstan friction, which describes how tension changes around a wrapped rope, with preslip contact stiffness, which describes resistance to tiny movements before full sliding. Together, these predict how the slip boundary shifts with vibration amplitude or holding tension.

2. Experimental apparatus and measurements

To test the prediction independently, I first measured geometry, vibration, friction and stiffness, then conducted 84 physical trials across 14 conditions, with six repeats each. Optical tracking measured movement along a four-wrap rope, and regression estimated local creep rates as vibration amplitude and holding tension were varied separately.

3. Identifying the slip boundary from creep profiles

For each trial, five descriptions of the movement pattern were compared using Bayesian Information Criterion, which penalises unnecessary model complexity. A boundary required a BIC advantage of at least 10, movement above three times the noise floor, and a position clear of the measurement edges.

4. Prediction accuracy and experimental validation

Across eight settings, the independently calibrated equation predicted the boundary angle with 6.7° root-mean-square error. Its agreement with measurements across both vibration and tension changes supports the force-balance explanation of where grip is lost under the tested conditions.

The measured dependence on log vibration amplitude was 9.54 ± 0.74 rad, compared with 10.10 ± 1.45 rad predicted. For log holding tension, the values were −5.56 ± 0.50 rad measured and −5.56 ± 0.62 rad predicted. Their agreement across two independently varied inputs supports the force-balance model under the tested conditions.

Physics-informed spirometry

1. Incomplete exhalations and missed obstruction

A spirometry test uses the amount of air exhaled in the first second (FEV₁) relative to the total forced exhaled volume (FVC) to help identify airway obstruction. If the patient stops blowing too early, FVC can be underestimated and the FEV₁/FVC ratio artificially increased, potentially concealing obstruction. I investigated whether the missing end of the exhalation could be estimated without treating every incomplete test as reliable.

I decoded NHANES flow–volume recordings at 100 samples per second, aligned the start of exhalation by back-extrapolation, and fitted a bounded two-compartment model representing fast and slow lung emptying. Complete recordings provided a reference for testing how accurately the model reconstructed the missing volume.

2. Reliability-based selection and model comparison

A fitted curve is not automatically a trustworthy clinical estimate. I therefore compared the physiological reconstruction with ridge regression and built a reliability selector using curve-fit quality, remaining volume change, recording duration and agreement between the two approaches.

At 75% coverage, the selector gives results for the three-quarters of recordings judged most reliable and flags the rest for repetition. I used participant-separated cross-fitting to keep a person’s records from appearing on both sides of model evaluation, then tested the frozen method on separate NHANES survey cycles.

3. Independent-cohort evaluation

On naturally incomplete recordings, reconstruction reduced missed obstruction from 42.7% to 6.7%. At 75% accepted-case coverage in independent NHANES Cycles E and G, sensitivity reached 96.0% and 96.1%, with specificity of 99.7% and 99.8%. Cohen’s kappa was 0.952 and 0.959, indicating high agreement beyond chance.

Compared at the same accepted-case coverage, the physics-informed selector improved kappa over a ridge-feature selector by 0.019 and 0.030 in the two cycles. Paired bootstrap intervals excluded zero, supporting the value of the physiological features for deciding which incomplete recordings could be assessed reliably.
