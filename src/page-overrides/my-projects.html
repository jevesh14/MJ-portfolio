1. AmbuGo

a. Ambulance delays and traffic priority

When an ambulance is delayed at signals, it loses time reaching a patient or hospital. In Delhi, the fleet grew from 155 to 261 vehicles, yet reported response time increased from 13 to 17.4 minutes between 2014 and February 2025. Delays in medical help are reported to cause 24,012 deaths daily in India alone.

A route that looks fastest under normal signals may not remain fastest once a junction is given ambulance priority, because the released traffic changes queues farther ahead. I developed AmbuGo to plan the route and signal interventions together, using existing automatic number-plate-recognition (ANPR) cameras and authorised signal-controller connections rather than a new roadside sensor network.

b. Routing, selective signal priority and driver override

The minimum-intervention joint controller compares routes together with the signal changes each would require. It first retains choices that meet a predicted ambulance-arrival allowance, then selects the one that causes less delay to other drivers. This is selective priority: the system requests enough intervention to keep the ambulance on schedule, while accounting for queues that those changes create elsewhere.

Minimum-green and clearance constraints preserve the legal signal sequence, while receiving-space checks prevent traffic being released onto a full road. Progress feedback increases priority if the ambulance falls behind; removing it added 38.82 seconds in the high-load test. A driver override lets crews change routes around a blockage, updating signal requests along the new path.

c. Traffic measures and simulation network

To measure disruption to other traffic, I counted additional vehicles on each road over time relative to routing alone. The total is expressed in vehicle-hours: ten extra vehicles present for six minutes contribute one vehicle-hour. This measures additional traffic accumulated across the network, rather than the delay of one car; waiting before network entry and unfinished journeys remain in the accounting.

d. Comparative traffic simulations and results

I compared controllers under matching traffic demand and observation errors through 14,400 mesoscopic and 1,728 held-out microscopic runs, with individual-vehicle tests in Eclipse SUMO. Routing alone leaves signals unchanged; aggressive priority uses stronger intervention to seek the fastest ambulance journey; selective priority limits intervention within its predicted arrival allowance.

Selective priority reduced mean journey time from 401.57 to 222.29 seconds, retaining about 94% of aggressive control’s time saving. It took 11.10 seconds longer than aggressive control while reducing excess road occupancy by 3.84 vehicle-hours, meaning less additional traffic accumulated on surrounding roads over the course of the simulation.

In the simulations, AmbuGo retained 94% of aggressive priority’s time saving for the ambulance while causing less traffic to build up on surrounding roads.

e. Operator dashboard and ambulance-driver interface

The two screens below show how AmbuGo is used: the traffic-control dashboard lists active ambulance missions and signal-priority requests, while the driver view shows the selected route and upcoming instructions.

f. Punjab pilot and expanded deployment

A separate 31-intersection Punjab pilot recorded approximately 32% lower emergency response time. Phase 1 expanded to 121 intersections in August 2026, supporting roughly 700 ambulance movements daily.

2. EcoFog

a. Water scarcity and fog harvesting

White · Hydrophilic capture layerBlack · Hydrophobic drainage support

More than 2 billion people live in water-stressed countries. Where freshwater is limited but fog is frequent, mesh collectors can catch airborne droplets and drain them into a container without pumping or refrigeration.

Conventional Raschel mesh is inexpensive, but water held between its fibres can block fresh collection. EcoFog places hydrophilic PVA/PEO capture fibres and a hydrophobic PLA drainage support in the same flat layer, coupling fine droplet capture with larger drainage paths. The contrasting wetting regions draw on a desert beetle, helping captured droplets merge and leave the surface instead of remaining trapped.

b. Mesh fabrication and material characterisation

I produced the fibres by electrospinning, where an electric field draws a polymer solution into fine strands across the support. I used titanium dioxide nanoparticles to provide droplet-formation sites, silica nanoparticles to reinforce the fibre network, and glutaraldehyde to create acetal crosslinks that stabilise the PVA fibres in water.

To examine how fabrication affected the material, I used field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) to assess fibre continuity and particle distribution, and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) to check changes in chemical bonding. The selected formulation contained 18 wt% polymer, a 90:10 PVA:PEO ratio and 1 wt% of each oxide filler.

To check whether the two surfaces behaved as intended, I measured water-droplet contact angles of 20.9–23.9° on the fibres and approximately 100° on PLA. The contrast supports their respective capture and drainage roles, independently of the collected-water measurements.

c. Formulation optimisation and TiO₂ loading

To determine how much titanium dioxide improved capture, I compared collection across different particle loadings. Collection peaked at 1 wt% TiO₂, then declined as microscopy showed less uniform fibres and particle clusters, explaining why adding more particles did not keep improving the mesh.

To explore which experiments could be most informative, I fitted Gaussian-process regression to 36 preliminary measurements. This estimates both collection performance and uncertainty between tested settings. The regression was exploratory; measured material and collection tests determined the reported formulation.

d. Outdoor water collection and performance

In simultaneous five-day outdoor testing, EcoFog collected 17.3 ± 2.1 L/m²/day, compared with 4.6 ± 0.51 for Raschel mesh. Testing bare PLA separately helped distinguish the capture layer’s contribution from the support alone.

An approximately 50% lower retained-water fraction showed that improved drainage accompanied the higher collected-water yield. Collection requires no grid power, and the composite-mesh work received an ISEF Third Grand Award.

3. PredictX

a. Access to diagnostic screening

An estimated 47% of the world’s population has little or no access to reliable diagnostic services. Where specialised tests are expensive or unavailable, routine laboratory measurements can help identify people who need further examination.

I developed PredictX to run independently trained diabetes, kidney, liver, cardiovascular and breast-cancer risk models through one report workflow. Rather than merging diseases into a single model, the architecture shares compatible laboratory processing while preserving each model’s required inputs and calibration.

b. Laboratory preprocessing and model performance

To prepare each report for the disease models, a canonical laboratory processor validates measurements, converts units and derives biochemical ratios. Compatibility-controlled reuse shares calculations only when feature order and fitted imputation, scaling and missing-value rules match, reducing repeated conversions by 80%. SHA-256 fingerprints verify model files; the FastAPI service handles batches of up to 128 records.

The table reports AUROC on held-out records, measuring whether disease cases receive higher scores than non-disease cases. A value of 0.5 indicates random ranking; 1.0 indicates perfect separation within that test cohort.

d. Physics-informed modelling of incomplete spirometry

Spirometry is a breathing test used to assess airway obstruction in conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). If a patient stops exhaling before the test is complete, the recorded total volume (FVC) is too small. This raises the ratio of first-second volume (FEV₁) to FVC and can hide airway obstruction.

To address this, I fitted a bounded two-compartment model to the recorded curve. Its fast and slow components represent different rates of lung emptying and estimate the exhalation missing from the recording. Agreement with ridge regression, a separate statistical estimate, and the quality of the curve fit then guide which screening decisions are reliable enough to retain.

In naturally incomplete tests, missed obstruction fell from 42.7% to 6.7%. The selector retains the most reliable 75% of recordings and withholds uncertain cases for repeat testing. Read the spirometry study

e. Clinical evaluation across five hospitals

Under Dr. Naresh Somani’s mentorship, I led evaluation involving 3,000+ people at five hospitals. Standard follow-up corroborated suspected conditions in approximately 65% of high-risk cases.

A ₹20 lakh CSR sanction supports further research and development.

4. AERIS

a. Speaker identification and vocal emotion recognition

Hearing aids can be expensive, with maintenance and eventual replacement adding to their cost. For people with hearing loss, a transcript offers another way to follow words but does not necessarily convey who spoke or the emotion in their voice. I developed AERIS to convey speaker identity and vocal emotion through an OLED wrist display and five distinct vibration patterns, providing visual and tactile cues alongside speech.

A Raspberry Pi 4 performs the audio analysis and sends labels over Wi-Fi to an ESP32 wrist unit. Separating recognition from the feedback hardware allows the wearable to focus on displaying labels and delivering tactile cues, rather than running the full models on the wrist.

b. Recognition performance and wearable testing

To distinguish four known speakers, I extracted 40 mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), which summarise the frequency structure of each audio sample, and trained a 1D convolutional neural network to recognise patterns associated with each voice. Training allowed up to 100 epochs, with a patience of 20 epochs for the validation-based stopping rule, so the network did not keep training indefinitely after validation performance stopped improving.

Emotion recognition runs separately through a pretrained Wav2Vec2 model, which extracts learned speech features from the audio waveform and assigns scores to neutral, happy, sad, angry and fearful speech. The highest-scoring label is sent to the wrist unit, where the OLED and corresponding vibration pattern communicate the inferred emotion without requiring the user to hear the voice.

The speaker model achieved 99.3% accuracy with a mean software response time of 250 milliseconds on the four-speaker task. Emotion classification used the separate pretrained model; these speaker results do not measure its emotion accuracy or recognition of previously unseen voices. After first-author publication at IEEE ICESC 2025, I developed the wearable extension and demonstrated it to children and families, connecting the software labels to a device they could see and feel.

c. Family feedback and recognition

In a translated testimonial, Basant Lal, the father of nine-year-old Savan Lal, described his family’s experience after AERIS was introduced at Mook Badhir Vidyalaya.

“Now Savan wears the watch and feels emotions through vibrations. It shows words in Hindi and English, so I can set it up easily even without knowing English.”

“I see my son smiling more now. He is more active.”

5. Project Niraksha

a. Vision-guided targeted crop treatment

Pest damage may be concentrated in parts of a field, while uniform treatment applies the same response everywhere. In Project Niraksha, I developed the image-analysis pipeline and connected detections to a location-specific, operator-controlled drone response.

To locate crop targets and distinguish affected regions, YOLOv8-s detects objects, DeepLabv3+ labels image regions, and DINOv2 supplies features for identifying unusual patterns. Environment-aware fusion adjusts their relative weights using light, temperature, humidity and pressure measurements. This combines complementary observations instead of relying on one visual model under every field condition.

A Raspberry Pi 5 runs perception separately from flight control, while HERE3+ GNSS associates observations with field locations. My contribution carried each detection through response logic to commands the physical platform could execute.

b. System integration and field trials

Assembly and farm trials checked the complete camera-to-response sequence under changing light and crop appearance, including operation by a village representative. The trials showed that observations could be carried through to an operator-controlled field response; detector accuracy and any reduction in chemical use still require separate quantitative evaluation.
