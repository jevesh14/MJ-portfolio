Human Archive

Business Growth & Machine Learning Data Intern · 18 May–29 June 2026

Hand-movement representations

When robots learn a task from a person’s demonstration, ordinary video shows what happened but does not directly provide the hand’s three-dimensional path or the timing of contact with an object. At Human Archive, I developed 3D hand-trajectory representations from recorded manipulation tasks, linking a physical action with the movement data needed to train robot-learning systems.

The paired demonstration shows the human action beside its reconstructed hand motion. This makes the hand’s kinematics—its movement through space and time—available alongside the original recording, rather than relying on video appearance alone.

Data quality and robot trials

I synchronised video, depth, motion and tactile streams so that hand position, object appearance and contact measurements referred to the same moment. I then checked the aligned recordings for timing errors and inconsistent motion before they were added to robot-learning datasets.

I also trained and evaluated physical robots on manipulation tasks, using failed grasps and incomplete executions to improve both the demonstrations and the quality checks. The resulting hand representations contributed to data products supplied to six AI laboratories.

Factory partnerships

Collecting demonstrations of real work required access to people and factory environments, not just a lab. I managed partnerships with 12 manufacturing facilities and helped onboard more than 100 workers to record authentic tasks.

During the six-week internship (18 May–29 June 2026), I worked across data collection, multimodal alignment and physical robot testing, connecting the recorded demonstrations to the requirements of downstream robot training.

Wink

Visitor detection and tracking

Shop cameras record where customers go, but watching hours of footage does not tell a retailer which displays attract attention or where people stop. I developed Wink to extract those patterns from existing cameras, using YOLO object detection and multi-object tracking to follow visitors across frames. Appearance-based person re-identification helps link observations of the same person, rather than counting every new frame as a new visit.

To relate those tracks to the shop itself, I used point-in-polygon tests to determine when a tracked position entered or left a defined aisle or display zone. The timestamps give dwell time, while transitions between zones show how people move through the store.

Behavioural measurements

These records feed a dashboard showing visitor counts, stopping locations, dwell times and movement between zones. A retailer can compare displays or time periods using the same tracking logic, without manually reviewing every recording.

Edge deployment

To make the system affordable for small stores, I moved the inference pipeline onto retailers’ own computers and reused the cameras they already had. In the deployment described here, reported monthly operating costs fell from about $500 to $5, while raw footage remained inside the store.

Professor’s Portfolio Website

Web developer · University of Pennsylvania, SP2

An academic website has to make years of research accessible to people who may be looking for only one paper, book or area of work. I designed and built Professor Femida Handy’s website around that need, organising publications, books, research areas and appointments into separate, easy-to-navigate sections rather than reproducing her CV as a single long page.

I handled the site structure, front-end implementation and revisions, incorporating Professor Handy’s feedback throughout and delivering the finished website in two weeks.
