Human Archive

Business Growth & Machine Learning Data Intern · 18 May–29 June 2026

Hand-movement representations

At Human Archive, a YC-backed robotics-data startup, I developed 3D models of hand movement from recorded human actions. The work turns a demonstrated manipulation into a movement representation that robotics researchers can connect to a physical task and use in robot-learning datasets.

The paired view shows the relationship directly: a person performs the task on the left, while the corresponding hand movement appears in three dimensions on the right. This connects the visible action to information about how the hand moves through it.

Data quality and robot trials

I worked on temporal synchronization and quality assurance across multimodal video, depth, motion, and tactile measurements. When a hand grasps or releases an object, those streams need to refer to the same moment. Video gives the task context, depth gives spatial geometry, movement describes the trajectory, and touch information records contact.

I also trained and evaluated physical robots on everyday manipulation tasks, checking grasp success and task completion. Unsuccessful grasps and incomplete executions guided revisions to demonstrations and data-quality checks, linking data preparation to observed task performance. The hand models formed part of data products commercially supplied to six AI laboratories, connecting the custom movement representations to downstream robot-learning datasets.

Factory partnerships

I managed partnerships with 12 manufacturing facilities and helped onboard more than 100 workers. This made authentic factory tasks available for collection and required coordinating facilities, participants, and recording operations alongside the technical representation work.

I completed the six-week hybrid internship as the youngest intern in the company’s history, working across robotics data, physical trials, and industry collection operations.

Wink

Tracking store visits

I built Wink to turn store-camera footage into measurements of how shoppers use a store. YOLO detects people in each frame; multi-object tracking connects their positions over time. Person re-identification uses appearance information to help relate observations of the same visitor, producing a movement history for a visit.

I then connected that history to the store layout. A shopper’s tracked position is represented as a point, and a point-in-polygon test checks whether it falls inside a defined aisle, display area, or other zone. Timestamped zone-entry and exit events measure how long a shopper stays in an area, known as dwell time. Zone changes describe movement through the store.

From movement to a dashboard

The dashboard combines these events into visitor counts, stopping locations, time spent near displays, and movement summaries. Keeping a visitor’s identity consistent matters: repeated frames of someone standing still should add time to one visit. The reporting layer turns those accumulated observations into patterns a retailer can compare across areas and time periods.

Running analysis in the store

I moved inference to the edge, running video analysis on retailers’ own computers and reducing the reported monthly operating cost from approximately $500 to $5 while keeping raw footage in the store. The retailer receives movement and dwell-time measurements from existing hardware. This connects the deployment architecture to two practical outcomes: lower recurring processing cost and local control of the video used to produce the analytics.

Professor Femida Handy’s academic website

Web developer · University of Pennsylvania, SP2
