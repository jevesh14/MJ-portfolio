Human Archive

Business Growth & Machine Learning Data Intern · 18 May–29 June 2026

Hand-movement representations

When a robot learns to pick up an object from a human demonstration, it needs the hand’s three-dimensional path and the timing of contact as well as the visible action. Ordinary video alone does not directly supply these movement and force measurements.

To make demonstrations more useful for robot learning, I developed 3D hand-trajectory representations at Human Archive, reconstructing how the hand moves through each recorded task. These trajectories describe the action’s kinematics, or movement through space and time, alongside its video.

Data quality and robot trials

Through temporal synchronisation of multimodal recordings, I aligned video, depth, motion and tactile streams so that hand position, object appearance and contact measurements referred to the same instant. Quality assurance then identified timing and movement problems before recordings entered the dataset.

Alongside this data work, I trained and evaluated physical robots on manipulation tasks, checking whether they could grasp objects and complete the demonstrated action. Unsuccessful attempts informed revisions to the demonstrations and the checks applied to new recordings.

Factory partnerships

The hand representations contributed to data products supplied commercially to six AI laboratories. To support collection from real factory tasks, I also managed partnerships with 12 manufacturing facilities and helped onboard more than 100 workers.

Across the six-week internship, the work linked recorded human tasks with the preparation of robot-learning data and physical manipulation trials. The paired demonstration shows the action beside its reconstructed hand trajectory, while the photographs show robots being tested on tabletop tasks.

Wink

Visitor detection and tracking

Although store cameras record shoppers, retailers still need to establish which displays attract attention and where visitors spend time. To obtain these measurements from existing footage, I developed Wink, which tracks visits and summarises activity within the store.

The software combines YOLO object detection, multi-object tracking to follow shoppers across frames, and appearance-based person re-identification to associate observations of the same visitor. Maintaining that identity prevents successive frames from being counted as separate visits.

To connect positions to the shop layout, point-in-polygon tests check whether each tracked position lies inside an aisle or display zone. The interval between zone entry and exit gives dwell time, while successive zone changes describe the visitor’s path.

Behavioural measurements

These measurements feed a dashboard combining visitor counts, stopping locations, dwell time and movement between zones. Retailers can therefore compare areas and periods without watching the full recording or manually counting visits.

Edge deployment

To reduce the cost of running this analysis, I moved processing onto retailers’ own computers and reused their existing hardware, reducing reported monthly operating costs from approximately $500 to $5 while keeping raw footage inside the store.

By connecting each visit with entry, movement, stopping and departure events, Wink turns camera footage into summaries of how different areas are used. The dashboard shows which zones attract visitors and how long those visitors remain there.

Professor’s Portfolio Website

Web developer · University of Pennsylvania, SP2

An academic website has to make years of research accessible to people who may be looking for only one paper, book or area of work. I designed and built Professor Femida Handy’s website around that need, organising publications, books, research areas and appointments into separate, easy-to-navigate sections rather than reproducing her CV as a single long page.

I handled the site structure, front-end implementation and revisions, incorporating Professor Handy’s feedback throughout and delivering the finished website in two weeks.
